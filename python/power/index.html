    Power and design analysis¶

    pilotr turns a specification into evidence for study planning: simulate from the ground truth, fit the analysis model and summarise across replicates. Alongside power it reports the Type S (sign) and Type M (magnitude, or exaggeration) errors of Gelman and Carlin (2014), computed over the significant replicates.

    Two-group Gaussian power¶

    power handles the two-group Gaussian design with one row per subject, using a two-sample t-test. It needs scipy (install the power extra):

    from pilotr import power

spec = {
    "name": "d", "seed": 1,
    "units": {"subject": {"n": 64}},
    "factors": [{"name": "group", "levels": ["a", "b"],
                 "contrasts": {"effect": [-0.5, 0.5]}, "between": "subject"}],
    "fixed": {"intercept": 100, "coefficients": {"effect": 5}},
    "response": {"family": "gaussian", "name": "score", "sigma": 10},
}

res = power(spec, n_sims=500)
print(table([{k: res[k] for k in (
    "power", "type_s", "type_m", "true_effect", "mean_estimate"
)}]))
    power type_s type_m true_effect mean_estimate
    0.504 0 1.4 5 4.99

    At roughly 50% power the Type M ratio is well above 1. Conditional on significance the estimated effect is exaggerated, even though the average estimate over all replicates is unbiased. This is the statistical-significance filter that design analysis is meant to expose.

    Power is a proportion over the n_sims replicates, so it comes with its Monte Carlo error. power_mcse is its standard error, and power_lo and power_hi bound its 95% Wilson interval, which stays between 0 and 1 and does not shrink to a point when power is 0 or 1. Type S and Type M rest on the significant replicates alone, so they settle more slowly than power. type_s_lo and type_s_hi bound a Wilson interval for Type S, whose plain standard error would be zero whenever no sign is wrong, and type_m_mcse is the standard error of Type M.

    print(table([{k: res[k] for k in (
    "power_mcse", "power_lo", "power_hi", "type_s_lo", "type_s_hi", "type_m_mcse"
)}]))
    power_mcse power_lo power_hi type_s_lo type_s_hi type_m_mcse
    0.0224 0.46 0.548 0 0.015 0.0205

    The t-test treats every row as an independent observation, which is valid only when each subject contributes one row and no rows share a cluster. Take 30 subjects crossed with 20 items, with by-subject and residual standard deviations of 1 and a by-item one of 0.3. With no true effect, a row-by-row test is significant in about half of all replicates, against the nominal 5%. power therefore refuses a design with an item unit, a within factor or a grouping factor besides subject, and its message names what it found. The R package's power_mixed() fits the model that such a design implies, as does power_mixed here when the design declares random effects. Continuous predictors and by-subject random effects are allowed, since with one row per subject they vary independently from row to row. Each level of the factor needs at least two subjects, so a design needs at least 4, and power refuses a smaller one, as the R twin's power_design() does.

    Directional predictions¶

    A preregistration that predicts the direction of an effect can test it one-sided. In power, power_curve and power_mixed, alternative="greater" tests whether the effect is positive and alternative="less" whether it is negative. The values are spelt as in the R package, so "two.sided" is the default, and scipy's spelling "two-sided" is refused. Here the second group is predicted to score higher than the first.

    one_sided = power(spec, n_sims=500, alternative="greater")
print(table([{k: one_sided[k] for k in ("alternative", "power", "type_s", "type_m")}]))
    alternative power type_s type_m
    greater 0.616 0 1.32

    The one-sided p-value comes from the same t as the two-sided one, and it is half the two-sided p-value when the estimate lies in the predicted direction. A one-sided test at α = 0.05 therefore rejects in exactly the replicates in which the two-sided test at 0.10 rejects with the estimate in that direction. Its power exceeds the two-sided power above. Type S and Type M are taken over the replicates significant in the predicted direction. Type S is therefore 0 by construction, and Type M is the exaggeration among the replicates that would support the prediction. power_mixed takes its one-sided p-value from the same Wald z, and its backend names the direction. Results from earlier versions were two-sided. In the direction of the true effect, a one-sided power at α is the two-sided power at 2α times one minus the Type S at 2α.

    Power over sample size¶

    power_curve sweeps the number of subjects and reports power at each:

    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from pilotr import power_curve, target_n

n_sims = 200
curve = power_curve(spec, subject_ns=[16, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128, 160, 192], n_sims=n_sims)
ns = [p["n_subject"] for p in curve]
pw = [p["power"] for p in curve]
# Each power estimate is a proportion over n_sims replicates, and each record
# carries its 95% Wilson interval, which the shaded band draws.
lo = [p["power_lo"] for p in curve]
hi = [p["power_hi"] for p in curve]

# The sample size the curve solves to, drawn as a vertical band. The next
# section explains the call.
solved = target_n(curve, target=0.8)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.4))
ax.axhline(0.8, ls="--", color="grey")
ax.axvspan(solved["lo"], solved["hi"], color="grey", alpha=0.12)
ax.axvline(solved["value"], ls="--", color="grey")
ax.fill_between(ns, lo, hi, color=BLUE, alpha=0.15)
ax.plot(ns, pw, "-o", color=BLUE)
ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.set_xlabel("$N$ subjects")
ax.set_ylabel("Power")
print(show(fig))

    The shaded horizontal band along the curve is the Monte Carlo interval of each power estimate, the 95% Wilson interval over its n_sims replicates that power_curve returns as power_lo and power_hi. The vertical band is the solved sample size, with its own interval.

    Each point of the curve draws its own replicates. Point j of a sweep over J sizes takes replicate_seeds(seed, J)[j] as its seed, so no two points share their random numbers, as target_n assumes when it fits them. Up to pilotr 0.3.0 every point took the specification's own seed, which seeds="shared" still does.

    The sample size the curve implies¶

    The point of a power curve is the sample size at which power reaches the target, and that is the number a preregistration quotes. Judging the crossing by eye gives a bare figure over points whose Monte Carlo intervals overlap. target_n fits the curve and inverts the fit, so the crossing arrives with the uncertainty that a simulated curve carries.

    solved = target_n(curve, target=0.8)
# n, n_lo and n_hi round up to whole subjects; lo and hi are the bounds before rounding.
print(table([{"target": solved["target"], "n": solved["n"],
              "n_lo": solved["n_lo"], "n_hi": solved["n_hi"],
              "lo": round(solved["lo"], 1), "hi": round(solved["hi"], 1)}]))
    target n n_lo n_hi lo hi
    0.8 137 128 146 127 145

    This design has a closed-form answer to check against. With an effect of 5 and a residual standard deviation of 10, power.t.test in R puts 0.80 power at 127.5 subjects in total, which falls inside the interval above. The interval spans some twenty subjects, the honest report at 200 replicates a point, and raising n_sims narrows it.

    Nothing is extrapolated. A curve that never reaches the target within the sizes swept is refused, and the refusal reports the range the sweep did cover. Had the sweep above stopped at 128 subjects, where power was still short of 0.80, that is what would have happened.

    solve_curve is the general form. It takes any curve with a decision rate against a swept value, so an effect-size sweep or a sweep over items is solved the same way, with transform="identity" for an effect-size axis.

    A sample-size justification¶

    A preregistration, a registered report or a grant application has to justify the sample size it asks for, and the solved size is only part of that justification. Lakens (2022, Table 4) also asks for the primary analysis with its alpha and sidedness, the desired power, each effect size with the reason it was chosen, and the power when the null hypothesis is true. A simulation adds items of its own: the number of replicates, the fits that were singular or carried a warning and the Monte Carlo error of every rate (Morris, White and Crowther, 2019). power_report writes all of them in Markdown from a power result and the target_n result. The example pairs the solved size with a run at that size, and adds the same design with no effect as null_result, whose rejection rate shows whether the test holds its level. The justification is illustrative.

    from pilotr import power_report

# The design at the solved size, and the same design with no effect.
at_n = dict(spec, units={"subject": {"n": solved["n"]}})
null_n = dict(at_n, fixed={"intercept": 100, "coefficients": {"effect": 0}})
report = power_report(power(at_n, n_sims=500), solved=solved,
                      null_result=power(null_n, n_sims=500),
                      justification="Half the residual standard deviation, the smallest "
                                    "difference worth acting on.")
print(report)

    # Sample-size justification

## Primary analysis

- Model and test: scipy.stats.ttest_ind, two-sample t with pooled variance
- Significance level: alpha = 0.05, two-sided

## Desired power and sample size

- Desired power: 0.8
- Sample size: 137 (95% confidence interval 128 to 146), the exact solution 136.19 rounded up
- Fit: a probit regression of `power` on the square root of `n_subject`, over 8 points from 16 to 192
- Heterogeneity factor: 1.00, the dispersion that scales the variance behind the interval, which is 1 where the curve fits the probit model

## Focal effects

The rates below are those of the simulated design, each over the replicates that returned an estimate for the effect.

### The difference between the two groups

- True value: 5
- Justification: Half the residual standard deviation, the smallest difference worth acting on.
- Power: 0.846 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.016; 95% Wilson interval 0.812 to 0.875), from 423 of 500 replicates
- Type S: 0.000 (95% Wilson interval 0.000 to 0.009)
- Type M: 1.092 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.013)

## Power when the null hypothesis is true

With every focal effect set to zero, at alpha = 0.05, two-sided:

- The difference between the two groups: 0.058 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.010; 95% Wilson interval 0.041 to 0.082), from 29 of 500 replicates

## Monte Carlo accounting

- Replicates: 500 simulated
- Null condition: 500 simulated

Each rate's Monte Carlo standard error is sqrt(p(1 - p) / n), over the n replicates that returned an estimate, and its interval is the 95% Wilson score interval. The standard error of Type M is the standard deviation of the exaggeration ratios over the square root of their number.

## Reproducibility

- Software: pilotr 0.3.0, Python 3.12.15, SciPy 1.18.1
- Specification: not given, so neither its seed nor its checksum is recorded

    Given spec_file, the path of the specification file the analysis read, the report also records the specification's seed and an MD5 checksum of the file's bytes. Each rate's Monte Carlo standard error and Wilson interval are computed in the report from its counts, and the R package's power_report() writes the same text, byte for byte, from the same counts. A power_mixed result is reported effect by effect. The R package's version also reports a precision_design() result, on the interval and the region of practical equivalence.

    Mixed-effects power¶

    power_mixed fits a mixed model with statsmodels (needs statsmodels and pandas), and the model comes from the specification, as in the R package. model_formula writes it in lme4's notation, character for character as the R package's model_formula() does, and model_data builds the columns it reads. Every coefficient is a fixed effect, an interaction "a:b" entering as its product column a_b. Every random intercept and slope the specification declares is a variance component, those of an extra grouping factor such as a site included. The response is analysed as log(y) for the lognormal family and as log(y - shift) for shifted_lognormal, whose coefficients are on the log scale, as in the R package. Every other family is analysed on its own scale. For bernoulli, poisson, ordinal and beta responses, that is not the scale the coefficients are written on, which is the logit scale, or the log scale for poisson. A linear model of a 0/1 response estimates a difference in probability, for instance, and setting it against the true coefficient says nothing about exaggeration. For these families, power_mixed warns and returns mean_estimate and type_m as nan. type_s compares signs alone, which the link preserves for a main effect, and power is the rate at which the linear model's test rejects. A bounded response can reverse the sign of an interaction between the two scales, so the type_s of an interaction is nan as well.

    Each random effect is fitted as an independent variance component. Where the specification correlates a grouping factor's intercept and slopes, the fitted model leaves the correlations out, and a warning names the grouping factors concerned. The R package's power_mixed() estimates them. A design without random effects is refused, since it leaves no variance component to fit. power analyses the two-group Gaussian design, and the R package's power_mixed() fits any other such design with lm() or glm(). Up to pilotr 0.3.0, the model here was the same whatever the specification declared, yv ~ cc with by-subject and by-item intercepts and slopes. It tested the first contrast of a single within factor crossed with items.

    The design below has one within-subject factor and crossed by-subject and by-item random intercepts and slopes. It also declares a correlation between each unit's intercept and slope, which the independent components leave out, so the call below warns about the correlations.

    from pilotr import model_formula, power_mixed

spec_mixed = {
    "name": "priming", "seed": 1,
    "units": {"subject": {"n": 12}, "item": {"n": 8}},
    "factors": [{"name": "condition", "levels": ["related", "unrelated"],
                 "contrasts": {"cond": [-0.5, 0.5]},
                 "vary_within": ["subject", "item"]}],
    "fixed": {"intercept": 6, "coefficients": {"cond": 0.1}},
    "random": {
        "subject": {"intercept_sd": 0.12, "slopes": {"cond": 0.04},
                    "correlations": {"intercept~cond": 0.2}},
        "item": {"intercept_sd": 0.08, "slopes": {"cond": 0.02},
                 "correlations": {"intercept~cond": -0.1}},
    },
    "response": {"family": "shifted_lognormal", "name": "RT",
                 "sigma": 0.3, "shift": 200},
}

# The model the specification implies, in lme4's notation.
print("`%s`" % model_formula(spec_mixed))
# A tiny replicate count keeps the docs build fast. Use 200 or more for real planning.
res = power_mixed(spec_mixed, n_sims=12)
print(table([{k: res[k] for k in (
    "n_attempted", "n_returned", "n_converged", "n_singular", "n_warning"
)}]))
print(table([{"effect": name, **{k: e[k] for k in (
    "power", "true_effect", "mean_estimate", "type_s", "type_m"
)}} for name, e in res["effects"].items()]))

    .y ~ cond + (1 + cond | subject) + (1 + cond | item)

    n_attempted n_returned n_converged n_singular n_warning
    12 12 1 11 0
    effect power true_effect mean_estimate type_s type_m
    cond 0.5 0.1 0.1 0 1.33

    res["effects"] holds one entry per focal effect, with the keys power returns. By default the focal effects are every coefficient of the specification, with the true values it gives them. focal chooses others: a list of names leaves their true values unknown, which leaves Type S and Type M undefined, and a dict from names to values gives the true values to compare with. A name is a column of the model, so "a_b" for the interaction "a:b". A coefficient that the specification does not list is not in the model, so a null effect is planned with a coefficient of 0. Results from earlier versions held one effect's numbers at the top level, as res["power"] and so on, for the first contrast of a single within factor. Those keys are kept for this release beside effects, and reading one raises a FutureWarning that names its place in effects.

    The priming design above is fully crossed, so each of the 12 subjects sees all 8 items in both conditions, 192 trials in all. A counterbalanced study, in which each subject sees each item in one condition only, has half the observations and lower power. From specification version 0.4, the specification writes it with "counterbalance": true on the condition, which rotates the conditions over subjects and items (see counterbalanced lists). The condition stays a within factor, so power_mixed here analyses the design as it does the crossed one. Releases that read no version above 0.3 take the two-list encoding among the worked encodings, which simulate produces exactly as the R package does. power_mixed analyses that encoding too, where the condition is the product column l_g of the encoding's interaction.

    The first table counts the fits as the R package does. n_returned counts the replicates that returned a fit, and each effect's power is the significant proportion among those that give it an estimate and a p-value. n_singular counts fits with a variance component on the boundary, judged by lme4's test of a standard deviation below 1e-4 of the residual one. n_warning counts fits whose optimisers did not converge or whose Hessian was not positive definite. A component at zero can fail statsmodels' check of the Hessian by itself, so in a singular fit that notice is left to n_singular. n_converged counts the fits that are neither singular nor warned. Given the model fitted here as its formula, the R package's power_mixed() reports the same five counts on this design. Here nearly every fit is singular, since 12 subjects and 8 items cannot support all four variance components when two of them are as small as these slopes. Such fits stay in power, as in the R package, because their fixed-effect estimates remain usable. statsmodels also notes that a fit may be on the boundary whenever a variance component is below 0.01. On the log scale every fit draws that notice, so it counts towards none of these.

    Every replicate here is analysed with the one model, singular or not. An analysis plan may instead preregister a simplification rule, such as dropping the slopes whenever a fit is singular, and such a rule has power and a Type I error rate of its own. It is simulated only in the R package, whose power_mixed() and precision_design() take a ladder of models as their formula and report the share of the replicates analysed with each model.

    Even at this tiny n_sims, the fixed effect is recovered (mean_estimate is close to true_effect). Each replicate is fitted by REML with Powell's method, and L-BFGS takes over if Powell's does not converge. statsmodels' default chain of optimisers stopped short of the optimum in most crossed fits, a failure that earlier versions of this page described as statsmodels overstating random-slope variance. Over the first 12 replicates of the crossed reaction-time example, the standard error of the effect now matches the one lme4 gives for the same independent components to four decimal places in 11. The same 6 replicates are significant in both. A replicate whose Powell fit raises is fitted again with the default chain and counts in n_warning.

    The fits take time, and more of it as the model grows. On a Windows laptop, with NumPy held to one thread, a replicate of the design above took about 0.3 s and one of the crossed reaction-time example (30 subjects, 24 items) about 2 s. One of the reading-time example, with 50 subjects, 40 items, seven coefficients and four variance components, took about 7 s. The random-effects design is held in sparse matrices from 100,000 entries, rows times random coefficients, which is quicker there and reaches the same optimum. workers spreads the replicates over processes (see Parallel execution).

    The p-values are Wald z tests. A Wald z treats the estimate over its standard error as normal and so ignores the uncertainty in the variance components. With few subjects or items, it rejects more readily than a test with Satterthwaite's degrees of freedom (Luke, 2017), and Satterthwaite's is the test the R package's power_mixed() applies. In small designs, the power here therefore tends to be higher than R's on the same data. In the example above, 6 of the 12 replicates are significant, against 4 in R. With 8 subjects, 6 items, random intercepts alone and no true effect, the test here rejected the null hypothesis in 0.070 of 300 replicates (Monte Carlo standard error 0.015). On the same data, the R package's Satterthwaite test of the same model rejected in 0.057 (Monte Carlo standard error 0.013), as the repository's tools/calibration/wald_null_rate.py measures. Data generation is identical across the two languages, so these differences lie in the model and the test alone.

    power_mixed carries its own simulation loop over the portable specification, with no other power package underneath it. It covers territory pioneered by simr (Green and MacLeod, 2016) and mixedpower (Kumle, Vo and Draschkow, 2021). pilotr differs in being driven by the portable cross-language specification, in reporting Type S and Type M errors alongside power and in built-in parallelisation.

    Which model each family gets¶

    The table sets each response family against the model fitted by this package's power_mixed and by the R package's power_mixed() and precision_design(). The R package chooses the fitter by the family, so that its estimates are on the scale the coefficients are written on. It fits a design without random effects, such as a between-subjects design with one row per subject, with lm() or glm(), and this package's power_mixed refuses one.

    Family R power_mixed() R precision_design() Python power_mixed
    gaussian, exgaussian lmerTest::lmer(), Satterthwaite t lme4::lmer(), Wald interval MixedLM, Wald z
    lognormal, shifted_lognormal as above, on the log scale as above, on the log scale MixedLM on the log scale, Wald z
    bernoulli lme4::glmer(), binomial, Wald z lme4::glmer(), Wald interval on the logit scale linear model on the response scale, a warning, no mean estimate or Type M, and no Type S for an interaction
    poisson lme4::glmer(), Poisson, Wald z lme4::glmer(), Wald interval on the log scale as for bernoulli
    ordinal, beta linear model on the response scale, a warning, no mean estimate or Type M, and no Type S for an interaction linear model on the response scale, a warning, no ROPE decisions (the interval width stays) as for bernoulli

    A Wald z with few subjects or items rejects more often than its nominal level (Li and Redden, 2015; Luke, 2017), so the power of a small accuracy or count design is somewhat overstated in either package. For ordinal and Beta outcomes, the R package's generate_design_analysis() writes a Bayesian design analysis on the logit scale of their coefficients.

    Parallel execution¶

    Every analysis on this page takes a workers argument that spreads the Monte Carlo replicates across local processes with concurrent.futures. Each replicate takes its own seed from replicate_seeds(), so the results are identical to a serial run whatever the worker count, and parallelisation costs nothing in reproducibility. The model fits dominate the running time, which makes the speed-up close to linear in the number of processes. power_curve starts one process pool and reuses it across the whole sweep.

    from pilotr import power, power_curve, power_mixed

power(spec, n_sims=2000, workers=8)          # same numbers as workers=1, sooner
power_curve(spec, subject_ns=[16, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128], n_sims=2000, workers=8)
power_mixed(spec_mixed, n_sims=500, workers=8)

    When calling a parallel analysis from a script on Windows or macOS, put the call inside an if __name__ == "__main__": block, the standard requirement for Python's spawn-based multiprocessing. Interactive sessions and notebooks need no guard.

    This design answers a serial bottleneck familiar from simr::powerCurve() in R, which pilotr's maintainer previously worked around by splitting the sample-size grid across separate jobs by hand and recombining the results afterwards (Bernabeu, 2021). In pilotr the same gain takes one argument.
